# Recency vs Frequency: Where We Are — speaker notes

## 1. Which memory wins?

A short recap of the question, then the formula derived in three steps, then what we found in the earlier rounds: why the formula works inside its band, why it fails outside, and the scorecard of our predictions. Then this week's experiment, which turns the band into a concrete range with a stated variability, and finally where the project stands.

## 2. Old and frequent, or new and rare?

The question came from Sir's Potter and Potyer example. When an induction head has seen a continuation often but long ago, and a conflicting one rarely but recently, which does it copy? Either answer can be right: if the world only has typos, frequency is right; if the world really changes, recency is right. So we built a task with both: each key's value is redrawn at rate h, and each copy is corrupted at rate epsilon. Because the data comes from known rules, there is an exact best answer, the Bayes predictor, and we can compare every trained model to it.

## 3. Two rules of the world, two stories for a probe

Every term in the formula comes from the two rules we used to generate the data. Rule 1, switches: each step the value stays with chance 1 minus h, or switches to a random one of 64 values. So no switch over G steps has chance (1 minus h) to the G, which we call s, and a switch lands on any particular value with chance 1 over V. Rule 2, typos: each time a value is shown it is shown correctly with chance 1 minus epsilon, or as the value plus one with chance epsilon. Now the probe: old copies of c, a gap, then recent copies of w, where w is c plus one, exactly what a typo of c looks like. Only two stories explain this well. A: nothing changed and the w's are typos, chance s times epsilon to the n_w. B: the value switched to w, chance (1 minus s) over V, and then the w's are clean copies, (1 minus epsilon) to the n_w. The old copies are clean in both stories, so they contribute the same factor to both, which is why they will cancel.

## 4. Divide the stories: time, prior and evidence

Which story wins? Divide A by B. That is the odds that the old value is still true; above one keep c, below one switch to w. The old copies cancel. Taking logs splits the odds into three parts. Time, which we will call the clock: how likely the old value survived the gap, which depends only on h and the gap. The prior: a switch only helps w if it lands on w, one time in 64. Evidence: each recent copy multiplies the odds by one minus epsilon over epsilon, nine at epsilon 0.1, which depends only on epsilon and the number of recent copies. Setting the log-odds to zero gives the switch rule; for small h times G it is the familiar form log(hG) greater than log V minus n_w log((1 minus epsilon) over epsilon). For example at h 0.01 and epsilon 0.1, one recent w should be ignored until the gap is about 200 pairs, two win after about 55, three after about 8.

## 5. Three terms we had dropped; with them it is exact

When we re-derived the formula, the two-story logic held, but three terms had been dropped. The biggest: a language model predicts the next token, and the next token is itself a copy that can be a typo. If the hidden value is c, the next token shows w with chance epsilon. So c leaks some of its chance to w and needs a head start just to tie: the hidden odds must be (1 minus epsilon) over (1 minus 2 epsilon), 1.13 at epsilon 0.1 and 1.75 at 0.3. Second, a switch can land back on c, with chance 1 over V, which adds one to the time part; this matters only when the evidence is weak. Third, in story B the old copies could all have been typos of c minus 1, which multiplies the evidence by one plus rho to the n_c; that is 1.11 for one old copy and essentially one for more. With all three, the formula matches an exact Bayes filter to 0.9 percent in the median over 920 cases; the old one was off by 47 percent. The conclusions do not change.

## 6. Models reason like Bayes, but with the wrong beliefs

Solid lines are trained models, dashed is Bayes, colours are the number of old copies. The lines for 2, 4 and 8 old copies nearly overlap, as Bayes says they should, but the switch happens at the wrong gap. The best description of every model is Bayes run with the wrong switch rate and the wrong typo rate, and those wrong beliefs follow two simple laws. When we say "the formula" in the rest of this deck, we mean this: Bayes with h-prime and epsilon-prime from these two laws. It was fitted on 12 settings, so it is an empirical law, not something we derived.

## 7. The time term is right; the evidence is under-weighted

Six things we knew before this week. The first three are the big picture: models look like Bayes, the formula predicts new models well and survives changes in task size, but not changes in position encoding or typo structure. The two graphs are the key finding. Bayes's rule has a time part and an evidence part, and we can test them separately. Left: with one old copy and no recent copies, only the time part is in play, and the model's belief that the old value still holds sits on top of Bayes's (1−h)^G at every switch rate. So the time part, which we call the clock, is exactly right. Right: all of the deviation comes from the evidence part. Each recent copy counts only w times as much as in Bayes, and w depends on the switch rate: about 0.15 at very low h, rising to about 0.7. Bottom: this explains the formula. The formula is a Bayes fit, and the only way a Bayes fit can say "each copy counts less" is to believe typos are more common than they are, which is the fitted ε′. That law has no h in it, so it applies one fixed discount everywhere, roughly the average w over the box we fitted on, about 0.25 to 0.65. So the formula works where w stays near that average and drifts outside.

## 8. At every edge w moves; the clock never does

The previous slide explained why the formula works inside the band. This one is the other side. At every edge of the band, the clock is still exact, and what changes is w. At high switch rates the slope is steep and w approaches one, so the believed typo rate drops toward the true one; the formula's typo law has no h in it, so it keeps predicting the in-band value and misses. At very low typo rates Bayes says each recent copy is very strong evidence, 2 to 4 nats, but the models give it much less. At very high typo rates it flips: Bayes says a recent copy is almost worthless, but the models still give it some weight and switch where Bayes never would; this regime barely affects the training loss, and two seeds with the same loss end up with very different w. The very low h edge is only partly explained; the scorecard on the next slide lists what we ruled out. The band is also tilted: at higher switch rates it needs a higher typo rate, which is why the high-h corner at low epsilon misses.

## 9. Scorecard: what we predicted, and how it went

Here is every prediction from the earlier rounds, each written down with a timestamp before we saw its result. Four passed: a switch-point law with the right clock and evidence at about a third predicted new models' switch points to within a factor of 1.3; how rare a conflict pattern is in training predicts how far models deviate from Bayes on it; w rises with h on models we had not looked at; and editing the ALiBi slope moves w in the predicted direction. Halving passed. Doubling raised w by 28 percent against the 30 percent we had registered, a narrow fail; the second seed, not separately registered, gave 37 percent. Six failed, and each taught us something: the clock is not the problem; extra conflict data helps but does not remove the deviation; context length, long gaps and training length do not set the low-h edge; RoPE is not immune; conflict data does not lift ALiBi at very low h; and the copy heads' attention is not where recent copies are counted. That last question is still open.

## 10. Turning "it works in this range" into a tested claim

Our goal this week was a concrete claim: the formula works in this range, with this variability. Our range from last time was honest but chosen after looking at the results. So this week we did it properly: we wrote down the range and what would count as passing, and timestamped it, before any model had finished training. Then we trained models at 24 settings nobody had used before, picked at random inside the range, three seeds each, and another set where we changed one training setting at a time. The formula was never refitted. Training three seeds per setting lets us measure how much models vary just from the random seed, which is the yardstick for variability.

## 11. The range holds: every check we committed to passed

The map shows the range as the dashed box. Squares are the settings from earlier rounds; circles are the 24 new ones, blue where the formula does as well as it does on its own fitting data, orange where it does worse. Across the 72 new models the formula, never refitted, explains a median 89 percent of the variation in the models' behaviour, with a typical error of 0.68 nats, and the models' believed switch rate lands within a factor of 0.8 to 1.7 of the formula's value for 90 percent of them. The five weaker settings sit at the two ends of the switch-rate range. The two at high h have fairly low typo rates, which fits the tilt of the band we found before. The three at low h we did not expect; our written expectation of where failures would be matched only one of the five.

## 12. The formula's error is a steady bias, about twice the seed noise

This is the "with this variability" part of our claim. A nat is a unit of error in log-odds: 0.26 nats means the odds are off by a factor of about 1.3, and 0.59 nats by about 1.8. Grey bars are how much two seeds of the same recipe differ: about a quarter of a nat. Orange bars are how far the formula is from the average model, with that seed noise removed: about 0.6 nats. So the formula is not as good as simply rerunning the training. But its miss is the same from seed to seed, so it is a stable, systematic description, which is what we will claim.

## 13. Robust to training settings; the evidence story replicates

Left: changing the learning rate, batch size, training length or making the model narrower leaves the formula's error within 7 percent; the wider model misses our limit by half a percent. Changing the architecture does matter, so we only claim the formula for two-layer attention-only models. Right: we also measured the evidence weight on the new models. The trend from last time comes back more cleanly, and the typical weight sits in the range the typo-rate law was fitted on, which is the explanation for why the formula works inside this range and drifts outside it.

## 14. A claim we can defend, and what is left

This is the claim we propose for the paper. Every number in it comes from models the formula never saw, graded against criteria fixed in advance. The story behind it: the models get the time part of Bayes exactly right and under-weight recent evidence by an amount that depends on the switch rate, and in ALiBi the positional slope is one real cause. The limits are part of the claim: the bias is about twice the seed noise, it is for two-layer attention-only models, typos in our data only go one way, and the very lowest switch rates are not fully explained. We think this is enough to start writing, and would like Sir's view on whether the RoPE test or a two-way typo version should come first.
