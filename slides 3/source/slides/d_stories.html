Background · deriving the formula, 1 of 3

Two rules of the world, two stories for a probe

Rule 1 · switches, rate h. Each step the value stays (1 − h) or switches to a random one of V = 64.
· no switch in G steps: s = (1 − h)G
· at least one switch: 1 − s  · lands on a given value: 1/V
Rule 2 · typos, rate ε. Each time a value z is shown, it shows z (1 − ε) or the typo z + 1 (ε).
· a copy of c shows c: 1 − ε; shows c + 1: ε
· n independent typos: εn
Probe: nc copies of c  →  gap of G pairs  →  nw copies of w = c + 1  →  which comes next?
w = c + 1 is exactly what a typo of c looks like, so the w's are either a real change or typos.
A · nothing changed; the w's are typos
no switch: s  ×  each w a typo of c: ε, nw times
P(A) ≈ s · εnw
B · the value switched to w
a switch landing on w: (1 − s)/V  ×  each w clean: 1 − ε, nw times
P(B) ≈ (1 − s)/V · (1 − ε)nw

The old copies of c are clean in both stories, so they count the same and will cancel. Other stories need an extra switch or extra typos, so they are much rarer (the ≈; we add the small ones back on slide 5).

Every term in the formula comes from the two rules we used to generate the data. Rule 1, switches: each step the value stays with chance 1 minus h, or switches to a random one of 64 values. So no switch over G steps has chance (1 minus h) to the G, which we call s, and a switch lands on any particular value with chance 1 over V. Rule 2, typos: each time a value is shown it is shown correctly with chance 1 minus epsilon, or as the value plus one with chance epsilon. Now the probe: old copies of c, a gap, then recent copies of w, where w is c plus one, exactly what a typo of c looks like. Only two stories explain this well. A: nothing changed and the w's are typos, chance s times epsilon to the n_w. B: the value switched to w, chance (1 minus s) over V, and then the w's are clean copies, (1 minus epsilon) to the n_w. The old copies are clean in both stories, so they contribute the same factor to both, which is why they will cancel.
