Recap · what we had established before this week

The time term is right; the evidence is under-weighted

1 · Bayes-shaped. Bayes with fitted beliefs best describes 75 of 84 models; extra old copies barely matter, as in Bayes.
2 · The formula predicts. New models at new settings: R² 0.87. Holds for context 192–512, V 32–128, K 8–32.
3 · Not universal. RoPE needs its own constants; with random typos, models act like plain counters.
4 · The time term is exact. Show one old copy and no recent copies, so only time matters:
model's belief "old value still holds"  =  (1−h)G
Dashed = Bayes, dots = models. They overlap at every switch rate, in all 124 models probed (RoPE too).
5 · Each recent copy counts only w×. The model's rule is Bayes with one extra factor:
log(s/(1−s)) + log V − w·nw·log((1−ε)/ε)
Bayes is w = 1. In ALiBi, w rises from ≈ 0.15 to 0.73 as h grows.
6 · Why the formula works where it does. Its typo law, log((1−ε′)/ε′) = 0.35 + 0.44·log((1−ε)/ε), is a w ≈ 0.44 averaged over the fitting box (shaded). Where the real w stays near that, the formula fits; where w drifts away, it misses.
Six things we knew before this week. The first three are the big picture: models look like Bayes, the formula predicts new models well and survives changes in task size, but not changes in position encoding or typo structure. The two graphs are the key finding. Bayes's rule has a time part and an evidence part, and we can test them separately. Left: with one old copy and no recent copies, only the time part is in play, and the model's belief that the old value still holds sits on top of Bayes's (1−h)^G at every switch rate. So the time part, which we call the clock, is exactly right. Right: all of the deviation comes from the evidence part. Each recent copy counts only w times as much as in Bayes, and w depends on the switch rate: about 0.15 at very low h, rising to about 0.7. Bottom: this explains the formula. Its fitted typo law is the same as multiplying the evidence by about 0.44, which is w averaged over the box we fitted on. So the formula works where w stays near that average and drifts outside.
