# Recency vs Frequency: Where the Law Holds: speaker notes

## 1. Which memory wins? Part 2

This talk continues from the progress talk. At the end of that talk you asked us four things: whether our formula and its derivation are right, where exactly the formula works, why it works there, and why it fails outside. This deck first recaps the earlier work in four slides, then answers those four questions in order. Everything here was tested with predictions written down before we looked at the results, and we show the failed predictions next to the passed ones.

## 2. Old and frequent, or new and rare?

A quick reminder of the question. Induction heads are the attention circuit that copies what followed a token the last time it appeared. Suppose the context has Harry Potter five times, long ago, and Harry Potyer once, just now. Which should the model copy? There is no single right answer: it depends on the world. If copies are sometimes corrupted by typos, the five old copies are better evidence and frequency wins. If names really change over time, the recent copy is the current truth and recency wins. So the right behaviour depends on two rates: how often values really change, and how often copies are corrupted. That is what makes the question precise enough to test.

## 3. A synthetic world where we know the right answer

The setup, in one picture. Training data are streams of key and value pairs: 16 keys, 64 values, 256 pairs per sequence, fresh random data every step so nothing is memorised. Each key has a hidden value. At every step it switches to a new random value with probability h, a real change. Each time it is shown, it can instead show a typo, the value plus one, with probability epsilon. Because we wrote the generator, an exact Bayes filter gives the best possible prediction. The measurement is the conflict probe at the bottom: n_c old copies of value c, then a gap of G pairs, then n_w recent copies of w = c plus 1, which looks exactly like a typo of c, then the query. We read off log p(c) over p(w) from the model and from Bayes. Positive means it trusts the old value, negative means the recent one. The models are small: 2 layers, width 128, 4 heads, about 150 thousand parameters, mostly attention-only, with a learnable ALiBi slope per head so we can read and edit how fast each head forgets.

## 4. Models reason like Bayes, with the wrong beliefs

What we had at the time of the talk. First, behaviour: when we fit candidate rules to each model's 112 probe answers, the Bayes rule with fitted beliefs h-prime and epsilon-prime wins for 75 of 84 models. That fit is informative: on random smooth curves the same Bayes family only reaches R squared 0.73, against 0.95 on the models. Second, the beliefs are wrong in a regular way. Models act as if values switch more often than they do, and they pull the typo rate toward the middle. Two formulas describe this, and they predict models trained at a setting they never saw with R squared 0.87. Third, a cause: when we rescale the ALiBi slopes of the copy heads after training, h-prime moves with the slope, and the slope training picked is exactly the loss minimum. In run 6 we froze the law and tested it on 63 new models. It held inside its range and across task sizes, but at only one of four settings outside its range. That is what you picked up on.

## 5. Four questions from the talk

These are the four questions you asked at the end of the talk, and the rest of the deck follows them in order. Question 1 is about the theory: the Bayes boundary in our paper and the fitted law. Question 2 asks for the exact region where the law works, which we had not mapped: run 6 tested only seven new settings. Questions 3 and 4 ask for an explanation of both sides, and you were clear that the point is to understand the behaviour, not to keep adding fitted terms. We worked in rounds. ## 6. Every term comes from two rules of the world

Before any formula, the two rules of our world, because every term in the derivation comes from them. Rule 1, switches: at every step the value stays the same with chance 1 minus h, or switches with chance h, and a switch picks a fresh value at random out of 64. So the chance of no switch over G steps is 1 minus h, multiplied G times: (1 minus h) to the G. We call that s. The chance of at least one switch is 1 minus s. And when a switch happens, the chance it lands on one particular value is 1 over V, 1 over 64. Rule 2, typos: every time the value is shown, it is shown correctly with chance 1 minus epsilon, or as a typo with chance epsilon, and a typo of z always shows z plus 1. So a copy of c shows c with chance 1 minus epsilon and shows c plus 1 with chance epsilon. Copies are independent, so several typos in a row multiply: epsilon to the n. These are exactly the rules that generated the training data, so the ideal Bayes reasoner knows them. Everything on the next slides is just these few numbers multiplied together.

## 7. Two stories can explain a probe

Now the probe. The model sees some old copies of c, then a gap of G pairs, then some recent copies of w, and has to say which value comes next. We choose w as c plus 1 on purpose: that is exactly what a typo of c looks like, so the recent w's could be a real change, or just typos. Only two stories explain this. Story A: nothing changed, and the w's were typos. From rule 1, no switch has chance s. From rule 2, each w being a typo of c has chance epsilon, and there are n_w of them, so epsilon to the n_w. Multiply: s times epsilon to the n_w. Story B: the value switched to w during the gap. A switch has chance 1 minus s, and it has to land on w, 1 over V. Then each recent w is a clean copy, 1 minus epsilon each. Multiply: (1 minus s) over V, times (1 minus epsilon) to the n_w. In both stories the old copies of c are clean copies, so they count the same in both. Any other story, like two switches, or some old copies being typos, needs an extra rare event, so it is much less likely. The approximately-equal signs are there because we will add two small corrections at step 5.

## 8. Compare the stories: where are they tied?

Step 3: which story wins? We take the ratio A over B. That is the odds that the old value c is still true. If it is above 1, story A is more likely, so keep c. If it is below 1, story B is more likely, so switch to w. Exactly 1 is a tie, and the gap where the odds pass through 1 is the switch point. That is why we set the odds to 1. Dividing also keeps only what differs between the stories: the old copies count the same in both and cancel. Doing the division: s over ((1 minus s) over V) is s V over (1 minus s); that is the time part, large when the gap is short and the old value has probably survived. Epsilon to the n_w over (1 minus epsilon) to the n_w is 1 over R, where R is ((1 minus epsilon) over epsilon) to the n_w; that is the evidence part, multiplying by 9 per recent copy at epsilon 0.1. Set the odds equal to 1 and rearrange: s over (1 minus s) = R over V. That is the formula in the paper. Example: at h = 0.01 and epsilon 0.1, one recent w is ignored unless the gap is about 200 pairs; two w's win after about 55; three after about 8. The number of old copies does not appear: old copies tell you what the value was, not whether it changed.

## 9. The model predicts the next token, and it can be a typo too

Step 4 is the subtle one, and it is the biggest thing the paper missed. Our probe does not ask the model "what is the hidden value?". A language model only ever predicts the next token, so we read its probabilities for the next token being c or w. But the next token is just another copy, and rule 2 applies to it too. If the hidden value is c, the next token shows c with chance 1 minus epsilon, and shows w, a typo, with chance epsilon. If the hidden value is w, it shows w with chance 1 minus epsilon; its typo would be w plus 1, which is neither c nor w. Adding up: the chance the next token is c is (1 minus epsilon) P(c). The chance it is w is (1 minus epsilon) P(w), plus epsilon P(c), the leak from c. The switch point for the model is where these two are equal. Set them equal, move the epsilon P(c) across, and you get (1 minus 2 epsilon) P(c) = (1 minus epsilon) P(w), so P(c) over P(w) = (1 minus epsilon) over (1 minus 2 epsilon). That is where both of those terms come from. In words: because c leaks some of its chance to w, c needs a head start just to tie. That head start is 1.13 at epsilon 0.1 and 1.75 at epsilon 0.3. Example at epsilon 0.3: even if c is 60 percent likely as the hidden value, the next token is w with 0.46 against c with 0.42.

## 10. Two small corrections, then solve

Step 5 adds the two small things we skipped at step 2, so that the formula is exact. First: when the value switches, the new value is random, and with chance 1 over V it is c again. Then the value is still c, which also counts for story A. So A's chance is s plus (1 minus s) over V, not just s. Dividing by B's prior, the time part becomes s V over (1 minus s), plus 1. Second: in story B, the old copies came from before the switch. They showed c, but the old value could have been c minus 1, with every old copy a typo, because a typo of c minus 1 shows as c. That adds a chance epsilon to the n_c. This cannot happen in story A, because there the value would still be c minus 1 now, and its typos would show c, not w. So the evidence part R gets multiplied by 1 plus rho to the n_c, with rho = epsilon over (1 minus epsilon). Now put it together. The odds are the new time part over the new evidence part. Step 4 told us the model's tie is where these odds equal (1 minus epsilon) over (1 minus 2 epsilon), not 1. Set them equal, subtract 1, divide by V, and you get the corrected formula. Both corrections are small: the plus 1 matters only when the evidence is weak, and 1 plus rho to the n_c is 1.11 for a single old copy at epsilon 0.1 and essentially 1 for two or more.

## 11. The logic was right; the corrected formula matches exact Bayes

So, is the derivation right? The two-story logic is right, and the paper's formula is what you get from step 3. The exact version adds the three pieces we just derived: the next-token head start from step 4, which is the biggest, and the two small corrections from step 5. To check, we compared both formulas with an exact Bayes calculation, a separate program that tracks all 64 possible values step by step with no approximations, on 920 cases over a wide range of h, epsilon and copy numbers. The corrected formula's switch gap is off by 0.9 percent in the median; the remaining error comes from the rare stories we dropped. The paper's formula is off by 47 percent, and by 77 percent when the typo rate is 0.3 or more, because the head start (1 minus epsilon) over (1 minus 2 epsilon) grows quickly as epsilon approaches one half. The conclusions do not change: old copies don't matter, and each recent copy moves the switch point earlier by a fixed step. Only the numbers change, mostly when typos are common.

## 12. The law itself is fitted, not derived

There are two formulas, and only one of them is derived. Prop. 2, the Bayes switch point, is derived, and we just corrected it. The law on this slide is not derived. It is a fit: we took each model's fitted beliefs h-prime and epsilon-prime, and fitted a power law and a straight line in logit space across 12 settings. Those 12 settings only cover one decade of h, 0.003 to 0.03, and typo rates from 0.05 to 0.3. So "is the law correct" really means "where does it describe the models, and why". That is what the next sections answer.

## 13. Freeze the law, train far outside its box, score it

To find the region we did the opposite of fitting. We froze the law with exactly the constants in the paper, and trained 50 new models at 25 settings far outside the box, two seeds each: switch rates from 0.0003 to 0.15, which is more than two decades, and typo rates from 0.01 to 0.45. Together with the older settings that gives 44. For each model we compare two errors over its 112 probe answers: the error of the frozen law, and the error of the best possible Bayes fit to that model, with the beliefs fitted freely. The ratio says how much worse the law is than the best a Bayes description could do. A ratio of 1 is perfect. Before the runs we said "works" means 1.25 or better. When the data came in, it turned out the law scores about 1.4 even inside the box it was fitted on, so 1.25 is stricter than the law is anywhere. So we judge it against itself: 1.5 or better, which is as good as it does inside its own box. We say clearly that this yardstick was chosen after seeing the data, and we report the strict version too: 8 of 44 settings pass it.

## 14. The h′ half travels; the ε′ half does not

This is the map. Panel a: each dot is a setting, coloured by the law's error over the best possible Bayes fit. Green is good. The dashed box is where the law was fitted. The green spreads around the box, down to h of 0.001, but it turns red at high h and at high typo rates. The two crosses on the left are h = 0.0003, where the models' switch points fall outside our probe window, so we cannot score them. Panels b and c split the law into its two halves. Panel b is the switch-rate half: believed over true switch rate, against h. The black line is the frozen law, and the models follow it over two decades of h, from 0.002 to 0.15. Only below 0.002 do they stop slowing down: h-prime levels off at about 0.01. Panel c is the typo half. The dashed lines are what the law predicts, flat in h because the law's epsilon-prime only depends on epsilon. The real models' epsilon-prime falls steeply as h grows, from about 0.33 to 0.09 at epsilon of 0.1. So almost all of the failure comes from the typo half of the law.

## 15. Where the law works, and where it fails

Here is the region as a table. The law works on a band: switch rates from about 0.001 to 0.045 with typo rates from about 0.05 to 0.25. That reaches half a decade below the range it was fitted on. It fails at high switch rates, from 0.06, or already from 0.045 when the typo rate is 0.1, with errors 2 to 5 times the best fit. It fails badly at typo rates of 0.35 and above, 3 to 15 times. It fails mildly at very low typo rates, 0.02 and below. At h = 0.0003 the models switch so late that the switch points fall beyond our longest probe gap, so the law cannot be scored there. Two things about the edges. They are stable: every new setting was trained with two seeds, and no setting changed sides. And the band is tilted, not a rectangle. At higher h it needs a higher typo rate: at h = 0.045 it works at epsilon 0.2 but not at 0.1, and even one of the original fitting settings, h = 0.03 with epsilon 0.05, misses. The reason for the tilt comes out of the explanation in the next section.

## 16. Bayes’s decision has two parts. Measure each one.

To explain why the law works, we went back to the derivation. Bayes's decision is a sum of two separate parts. The time part says how likely the old value is to have survived the gap. It depends only on h and the gap. The evidence part says how strongly the recent copies argue that the value changed. It depends only on epsilon and how many recent copies there are. Bayes simply adds them. A fitted law in h-prime and epsilon-prime mixes the two up, so we measured each part directly in every model. For time we built a new probe with no conflict at all: show a value once, wait d pairs, and ask the model how much it still believes that value. Comparing that with Bayes tells us whether the model's clock is right. For evidence we look at how far the switch point moves when we add one more recent copy, and compare with how far Bayes moves it.

## 17. Every model’s clock is right

First the time part. We show the model a value once, d pairs back, and read off how much probability it still gives to that value, corrected for typos. The dashed line is exact Bayes, which is just (1 minus h) to the power d. Left: very rare switches, so the belief stays near 1 for 250 pairs. Middle: h = 0.01, the belief fades over about 100 pairs. Right: h = 0.1, it is gone after about 30 pairs. In each case the model sits on the Bayes curve, ALiBi and RoPE alike. This holds in all 124 models we probed, across the whole map, three position encodings, and also in the models trained with extra conflict data. The median error is at most 0.04. It holds even at the settings where the law fails, so the law's failures cannot come from the model's sense of time. That also ruled out our first hypothesis, that ALiBi's straight-line distance penalty bends the clock: it does not.

## 18. Recent copies count for less, and h sets how much

Now the evidence part. For each model we find the gap where it switches from the old to the new value, for one, two, three, four and more recent copies. Each extra copy moves that switch point. We divide by how far the same extra copy moves Bayes's switch point, and call that w, the evidence weight. w = 1 means a recent copy counts exactly as much as Bayes says; w below 1 means it counts for less. The table gives the ALiBi medians: about 0.15 at the lowest switch rates, rising to about 0.7 by h = 0.06 and about 1 near h = 0.1. RoPE models give recent copies more weight and reach Bayes by h of about 0.01, but they also drop at very low h. So the models are not uniformly wrong about evidence: the weight they give it depends on the switch rate they were trained on. Bayes's evidence weight does not depend on h at all.

## 19. Why the law works inside the band

Putting the two measurements together answers question 3. The clock is right, so the models switch on roughly the right time scale. But they give recent copies only a fraction w of the weight Bayes gives them, and that fraction depends on h. Now think about what a Bayes fit can do with that. It has only two beliefs to adjust, h-prime and epsilon-prime. There is no parameter that says "count each copy at half strength". The only way to make recent copies count less in Bayes is to believe that typos are more common, because then a recent copy is more likely to be a typo. So under-weighted evidence shows up as a higher epsilon-prime, and switching too early shows up as a higher h-prime. Inside the box we fitted on, w stays in a fairly narrow range, about 0.25 to 0.65, so a single formula for epsilon-prime in terms of epsilon captures it well. That is why the law works there. It is a good summary of right clock and under-weighted evidence, but it is not the mechanism, and it holds only as long as w stays near the value it had inside the box.

## 20. One slope does two jobs

Why does w go up with h? For ALiBi models we found a mechanism. Each copy head has one learned number, its slope, which sets how fast its attention to a past copy fades with distance. Panel a: training sets that slope so the clock is right, so models trained with more switches get steeper slopes, very consistently. Panel b: the same slope also decides how much each extra recent copy tips the balance, so w rises with the slope. In Bayes, time and evidence are separate knobs; in an ALiBi head they are tied through one number. Panel c is the causal test. We took 50 trained models we had not used before and edited the slope by hand. Halving it made w fall to about 0.57 of its value, in almost every model. Doubling it raised w by about a third. We had pre-registered at least 30 percent for doubling; seed 0 gave 28 percent, a narrow miss, and seed 1 gave 37 percent. So this is a real causal channel. But it is not the whole story, because RoPE models, which have no slope at all, also give recent copies little weight at very low h.

## 21. At every edge w moves; the clock never does

Question 4. At every edge of the region, the clock is still right, and what changes is w. At high switch rates the slope is steep and w approaches 1, so recent copies get almost full Bayes weight and the believed typo rate drops toward the true one. The law has no h in its typo part, so it keeps predicting the in-box value and misses. At very low typo rates, Bayes says each recent copy is very strong evidence, 2 to 4 nats, but the models give it much less, so they look as if they believe in more typos. At very high typo rates it flips. Bayes says a recent copy is almost worthless, because it is nearly as likely to be a typo, so Bayes never switches inside our window. The models still give each copy some weight, so they switch, and no Bayes curve can do that. This regime barely affects the training loss, and it shows: two seeds at h = 0.01 and epsilon = 0.35 reach the same loss, but one weighs recent copies more than Bayes and the other almost ignores them. So the direction is explained, but the exact behaviour depends on the seed. The last row, very low h, is the one we could only partly explain; the next slide lists what we tested.

## 22. What we ruled out at very low h

The low-h edge is where we were least successful, so here is everything we tested, each with a prediction written down first. Maybe the clock breaks at long gaps? No: survival probes show it is exact out to 248 pairs, even at h = 0.0003. Maybe the context is too short to see a switch? Doubling the context moved the switch point by only 13 to 34 percent. Maybe training has too few long gaps? Using more keys gives 5 to 9 times more long gaps, and the switch point barely moved. Maybe the models are undertrained? Four times longer training left w unchanged at the lowest h. Maybe it is ALiBi's slope? RoPE models have no slope, and their w is also low at very low h. Maybe conflicts are too rare in training to matter for the loss? Adding 10 percent conflict sequences doubled RoPE's w at h = 0.0003, but left ALiBi's exactly where it was. One caveat: those conflict sequences only reach gaps of 128, while Bayes switches much later at this h. Last, maybe the copy heads count recent copies by how much attention they give them? We gave one recent copy the attention of four copies, and that reproduced only 11 percent of the effect of four real copies, so the count is made somewhere else in the network. What is solid: the clock is exact, w is low for both encodings, and for ALiBi at h = 0.0003 nothing we tried moved it.

## 23. What we predicted in advance, and how it went

Here is the full scorecard of what we predicted before seeing results in this round of work. Four predictions passed. A switch-point law, which says models get the time scale right but weigh evidence at about a third, predicted the switch points of the new models to within a factor of about 1.3. How often a conflict pattern occurs in ordinary training data predicts, on unseen models, how far the model deviates from Bayes on that pattern. The rise of w with h held on models we had not looked at. And slope surgery moved w in the predicted direction, with doubling just missing the size we set. Six predictions failed, and each failure taught us something: the clock is not the problem; extra conflict data helps but does not remove the deviation; context length, long gaps and training length do not set the low-h edge; RoPE is not immune; conflict data does not lift ALiBi at very low h; and the copy heads' attention is not where the copies get counted. We keep all of these in the record.

## 24. Answers to the four questions

To sum up. One: the Bayes derivation's logic is right, but our formula missed three terms; the corrected one matches exact Bayes to within 1 percent, and the conclusions stand. The law for h-prime and epsilon-prime is a fit, not a derivation. Two: the law works on a band of switch rates from about 0.001 to 0.045 and typo rates from about 0.05 to 0.25, tilted so that higher switch rates need higher typo rates, and these edges do not move with a second seed. Three: inside the band it works because the models get time exactly right and give recent evidence a reduced weight, and within the fitting box that weight stays close to the average the law encodes. In ALiBi one slope sets both the clock and the weight, which is why the weight follows h. Four: outside the band the weight moves away from that average, and the law has no way to follow it. Two edges are fully explained, the high-typo edge is explained in direction but depends on the seed, and the very-low-h edge is only partly explained. The next experiment is to switch off one head at a time on the probes and see which heads carry the count of recent copies.
