Which memory wins?

Recency vs frequency in induction heads:
the idea, the tests, and what we found

Nirbhay Sharma · Gathik Jindal · 6 October 2026

Progress update on the recency-vs-frequency project: where the idea came from, what we built, what we tested, what holds up, and whether we are ready to write the paper.

The idea

Old and frequent, or new and rare?

Harry Potter  ×5, long ago
· · · much later · · ·
Harry Potyer  ×1, just now
· · ·
Harry ___ ?

If the world has typos

“Potyer” is a slip. Trust the many old copies and answer Potter. Frequency wins.

If the world changes

The name really changed. Trust the latest copy and answer Potyer. Recency wins.

Induction heads are the attention circuit that copies “what came after this token last time”. When past copies disagree, which one do they copy, and is that a sensible choice?

The whole project is one question. A language model sees a name many times long ago and a slightly different spelling once, recently. Whether it should trust the old or the new copy depends on the world: typos favour the old, frequent copy; real changes favour the recent one. Induction heads do this copying in transformers, so we asked what rule they use.

The idea · what we expected

Two candidate rules, and our bets

Counter rule (Prop. 1)

What one attention head naturally does: every past copy is a vote that fades with distance.

→ More old copies always pull harder toward the old value.

Ideal rule (Bayes, Prop. 2)

Weigh “typo” against “real change”. Switch to the recent value when

log(h·G) > log V − nw·log((1−ε)/ε)

→ After 2 old copies, extra ones don’t matter. The gap and the recent copies do.

Bets written down before running: (a) 2-layer attention-only models count old copies  ·  (b) MLPs or a 3rd layer move them toward Bayes  ·  (c) the heads’ fading rate rises with the switch rate h

h = switch rate · ε = typo rate · G = gap since the old copies · nw = number of recent copies · V = 64 possible values

Two rules make different predictions. A counter, which is what a single head can compute, keeps getting more confident as old copies pile up. The exact Bayes answer stops caring after two old copies: what decides is how long ago they were (G) and how many recent copies there are. We wrote down three bets before running anything.

Sequences

256 key→value pairs. 16 keys (some common, some rare), 64 values.

Two kinds of noise

Each key’s hidden value switches with chance h, or shows a typo (value+1) with chance ε.

Never repeats

Fresh random sequences at every training step, so nothing can be memorised.

Exact answer key

An exact Bayes filter gives the ideal prediction at every position.

Worlds: 12 mixes of h ∈ {0.003, 0.01, 0.03} and ε ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3}, plus typos-only, switches-only and random-typo corners.

We generate our own data so that we know the right answer exactly. Each key has a hidden value. Occasionally it switches to a new value (rate h); occasionally it is shown with a typo (rate ε). Every training step uses brand-new sequences, and an exact Bayes filter tells us what an ideal predictor would say at every position.

The probe puts the two kinds of evidence head to head. A fresh key τ is shown with value c a few times, then other keys for a gap of G pairs, then with value w a few times, then queried. We read off how much the model prefers c over w, and compare it with the exact Bayes answer. Varying the three knobs gives 112 probe types.

What we ran

The main tests in plain words

TestThe questionHow
1 · Conflict probesWhich copy does the model trust, versus the ideal?112 probe types per model, compared with exact Bayes
2 · Rule fittingWhich formula best describes its choices?Counter rules vs Bayes with free beliefs (h′, ε′), scored on held-out probes

Six kinds of test. The probes measure behaviour. Rule fitting asks which formula explains it. Controls rule out the obvious artifacts. Slope surgery is the causal test. The mechanism work looks inside. The audit checks that everything we report is real.

Result 1 · behaviour

Models reason like Bayes, but with the wrong beliefs

Bayes with fitted beliefs is the best rule for 75 of 84 models (24 of 24 in the lr/batch sweep)
Not just a flexible fit: R² 0.95 on models vs 0.73 on random smooth curves and 0.74 on random counter mixes
But the beliefs are off: models act as if switches are more common (h′ > h) and typo rates sit near 0.25
Solid lines are trained models, dashed is exact Bayes, colours are the number of old copies. Top row is world C (h = 0.01, ε = 0.1), bottom row world D (h = 0.03, ε = 0.2). The models have Bayes's shape: lines for 2, 4 and 8 old copies nearly overlap, as Bayes says they should. But the switch from old to recent happens at the wrong gap and too sharply. The best description is Bayes run with the wrong h and ε. That fit is much better on real models than on random curves or random sums of counters, so it is not just flexibility.

Result 2 · a predictive law

The wrong beliefs follow two simple laws

Believed switch rate

h′ = 1.50 · h0.74

Always above the true rate in our range

Believed typo rate

logit ε′ = −0.33 + 0.44 · logit ε

Pulled toward about 0.25 whatever the true rate

Fit on 11 worlds, predict the 12th from (h, ε) alone: R² 0.87 (target set in advance: ≥ 0.75)

Each recent copy counts about half as much as Bayes says (median 0.46×). Scope: 2-layer ALiBi models, width 128; 95% intervals in the paper.

Given only the world's true switch and typo rates, these two formulas predict how a trained model will behave. We tested that by leaving one world out, fitting the laws on the other eleven, and predicting the held-out world's trained models: R² 0.87. Confidence intervals: 1.50 [1.29, 1.78], exponent 0.74 [0.71, 0.78], intercept −0.33 [−0.47, −0.04], slope 0.44 [0.35, 0.57].

Result 3 · cause

Fading rate sets the belief, and training chose it

Turn the slope knob after training: h′ moves with it, h′ ∝ sk, k ≈ 1.1–1.3 (monotone in 56 of 57 models)
The trained slope is the loss minimum in 85 of 85 models. A calibrated slope would cost +0.044 nats
So the miscalibration is what training prefers, not a failure to train. Removing late heads removes the decision (−96%)
This is the causal test. ALiBi gives each head a learned fading rate (slope). After training we scaled it up or down and measured the model's beliefs again. A faster fade makes the model believe switches are more common, in a near power law. On the right: ordinary-data loss is lowest exactly at the slope training found, in every one of 85 models. So the wrong belief is not slack; it is the loss-optimal setting for these heads.

Result 4 · inside the model

Copy heads are fading counters that add up

logit(c) − logit(w) ≈ Σheads γh · (Aold c − Arecent w)

2–3 copy heads; R² 0.90–0.99 across models; fade = learned slope (r = 0.990)

Open question: each head’s attention split is already Bayes-shaped and is computed inside the layer-1 copy heads. We have not found how yet.

Inside the model the decision is simple: a few copy heads each look back at old and recent copies, their attention fades at the learned ALiBi slope, and their outputs add up. That sum predicts the model's choice almost perfectly. What we cannot yet explain is how the heads' attention ends up Bayes-shaped; it survives removing every layer-0 head except the previous-token head.

Honest status

What failed, or changed our story

No position encoding (NoPE) models did not learn the task in our budget.
First ablation test had a bad control. We dropped it; the causal claim rests on slope surgery.
“Adding counters gives Bayes” is false: random counter mixes fit Bayes no better than random curves.
Counter formulas, even with content terms, do not predict behaviour.
Two path-removal predictions failed: the Bayes shape survives cutting the earlier heads we suspected.
One head’s slope does not track h inside the mixed worlds (R² 0.03); only between extremes.
Audit corrections: each recent copy carries about half the Bayes weight (we had said a third); the miscalibration is loss-optimal (we had said “cheap”). Both are fixed in the reports and the draft.
We report failures as clearly as successes. Several predictions we made did not hold, and two earlier claims had to be corrected by the audit. All of these are in the paper's limitations and appendix; none of them undercut the main result.
